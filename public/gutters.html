Roll-formed gutters

K-style · half-round · box · euro box · fascia · straight-face — in aluminum, painted steel, Galvalume, paint-grip and copper, rolled in the shop or seamless on your job site

Gutter is the part of a metal package most likely to send you looking for a second supplier. A shop that rolls beautiful standing seam often runs exactly one gutter profile, and the profile the architect drew is not it. RoofCoil indexes shops by the gutter profile and the gutter material they actually run, so you can find the one shop that has the machine instead of calling nine that don't.

Gutter profiles you'll find

5″ K-style

The residential default across most of the country. The ogee face reads as crown moulding from the ground, and it carries more water than a half-round of the same nominal size.

6″ K-style

A substantial capacity jump over 5″, and the right call on large or steep roofs, metal roofs that dump fast, and anywhere rainfall comes hard. Usually paired with 3×4 downspout.

7″ K-style

Light commercial and very large residential. Fewer shops have the machine, which is usually what sends people searching.

5″ half-round

The traditional profile, and the one historic districts specify. No flat back to fasten through, so it hangs on brackets and needs more of them.

6″ half-round

Closer to a 5″ K-style in capacity than its number suggests — size up a nominal inch when you switch profiles on the same roof. Common in copper.

6″ box

A squared trough with a flat face. Takes more water than a K-style of the same width and suits a contemporary elevation where the ogee face would read wrong.

Commercial capacity on a hung gutter. Where the engineer sized the run rather than the installer picking a profile.

Large commercial and industrial. Heavy enough that the hanger spacing and the bracket detail matter as much as the gutter.

6″ euro box

A box with a rolled bead at the top of the face. The bead is the look, and it is also what keeps a long straight face from oil canning.

Fascia gutter

Deep face that serves as the fascia itself, fastened to the rafter tails with no separate fascia board. Standard on a lot of western work and on open-eave framing.

Straight-face / square

A flat face with no bead and no ogee — the plainest profile, and the one modern elevations ask for. Shows every hanger and every dent, so gauge matters more here.

Sizing, in the order it actually gets decided

Three things set the gutter size, and the one people skip is the third. Roof area draining into that one run — not the whole roof, the run. Rainfall intensity where the building is, which is a local number, not a national one. And roof slope, because a steep roof throws more water at the eave than its footprint suggests, and a metal roof sheds it faster than shingles do.

Then the part that gets blamed on the gutter: outlet count. A correctly sized gutter with too few downspouts still overflows, and it overflows at the far end of the run where nobody is looking. Adding an outlet is cheaper than going up a profile size and usually solves the same complaint.

On commercial low-slope work the gutter is not just a shape — a hung gutter at the perimeter may have to be a tested assembly under GT-1, and fabricating it differently from what was tested takes the listing with it. Worth settling before the shop runs it rather than when the inspector asks.

Seamless, or the exotic profile nobody runs

Every joint in a gutter is a seam that will eventually leak, so a run that comes off a portable machine at the job in one continuous piece is worth the trip charge. Quick answers

What size gutter do I need?

Three things set it: the roof area draining into that run, the rainfall intensity where the building is, and the roof slope — a steep roof throws more water at the gutter than its footprint suggests. 5″ K-style is the residential default and 6″ moves substantially more water, which is the right call on large or steep roofs and in high-intensity rainfall regions. Downspout count matters as much as gutter size: a correctly sized gutter with too few outlets still overflows at the ends of the run.

Is half-round the same capacity as K-style?

No. A half-round carries less water than a K-style of the same nominal width because the cross-section is smaller — a 6″ half-round is closer to a 5″ K-style in capacity. Half-round also needs more hangers, since there is no flat back to fasten through. Size up a nominal inch when switching from K-style to half-round on the same roof.

What is the difference between .027 and .032 aluminum gutter coil?

Thickness, and what that buys is stiffness. .032 holds a straighter face over a long run, dents less from ladder contact, and is the usual choice on 6″ and larger profiles and on commercial work. .027 is standard for 5″ residential K-style. Both come painted with the same finish systems.

Can I put a copper gutter under a Galvalume roof?

No. Runoff off copper will corrode Galvalume, and the gutter is downhill of the whole roof. Copper gutters belong under copper, lead-coated copper, or a roof whose runoff never touches them. The same caution applies to a copper gutter hung with steel hangers or fastened with steel screws.

What is on-site gutter roll forming?

A portable machine brought to the job that rolls the gutter to the exact length of the run, so a 60-foot eave gets one continuous piece with no joint in it. Every joint in a gutter is a seam that will eventually leak, so a seamless run is the point. Shops with a portable gutter machine are marked in the directory under on-site gutter roll forming.
